\begin{abstract}
This draft literature review is structured for a thesis on a UR5e-based macro-micro robotic system with a flexible continuum tip for minimally invasive surgery (MIS). It consolidates relevant findings from prior work in the \textit{Lachlan Report}, identifies which foundations remain valid, and highlights where the state of the art now requires updated evidence. The review prioritizes three technical threads central to this thesis: (1) tendon-driven continuum manipulator design and motion algorithms, (2) integration with a macro robot (UR5e) with improved teleoperation behaviour, and (3) rapid end-effector exchange mechanisms. It also includes explicit ``missing area'' headings to direct next-stage searching, reading, and synthesis.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\subsection{Project Context and Problem Significance}
This thesis extends prior macro-micro robotic surgery work by coupling a UR5e macro manipulator with a new flexible continuum tip, then improving movement algorithms inherited from earlier implementations. The applied significance is strong: MIS demands dexterity in constrained spaces, low tissue interaction force, high positional repeatability, and robust safety controls.

From the earlier project, the macro-micro concept and teleoperation feasibility are already established at a proof-of-concept level. The key opportunity now is to move from demonstration toward a more clinically credible engineering argument through stronger modeling, improved control reliability, and modular hardware architecture (rapid tool exchange).

\subsection{Working Thesis Gap Statement (Draft)}
Current literature and prior project work support feasibility of macro-micro MIS manipulation, but leave a practical translation gap at the intersection of \textbf{accurate continuum movement control}, \textbf{stable and reliable teleoperation}, and \textbf{fast, repeatable end-effector reconfiguration}. This thesis addresses that gap on a UR5e-based platform by improving inherited motion algorithms and integrating reliability and modularity mechanisms necessary for repeatable experimental deployment.

\section{Literature Review}

\subsection{Introduction to Minimally Invasive Surgery (MIS) and Robotics}
Laparoscopic surgery is a core MIS approach in which instruments are inserted
through small abdominal incisions using trocar access ports, rather than a
large open incision (Ko et al., 2025). In this report, MIS denotes procedures
that use these reduced-access pathways to lower tissue trauma and recovery
burden relative to open surgery.

\begin{itemize}
    \item The clinical motivation: Why do we need surgical robots? (Precision, dexterity, patient outcomes).
    \item The rise of Macro-Micro systems (combining a large positioning arm like a UR5e with a delicate continuum tip).
\end{itemize}
\paragraph{Historical progression of surgery and MIS robotics}
Technology and surgery have been inseparable for centuries as every surgical advancement or care improvement has been enabled by the parallel development of instruments and tools. 
From anaesthesia to antiseptics, innovation has enabled surgeons to reduce risk and expand the scope of treatable conditions (Emerging Robotic Platforms for Minimally Invasive Surgery)
In the 19th century, innovations such as the transition from candlelight to electric lighting allowed for practical internal visualisation, marking the beginning of a major paradigm shift in the surgical field (Hargest five thousand years).
Electricity also enable the development of various specialised surgical procedures, such as electrocautery for haemostatasis and the use of electric drills for orthopaedic surgery (hargest 5000 years).
These innovations allowed for a stronger focus on precision and dexterity, and techniques such as laparoscopy and endoscopy were developed at the beginning of the 20th century, resulting in some of the first minimally invasive surgeries (MIS)(Hatzinger M, Fesenko A, Sohn M. The first human laparoscopy and NOTES operation: Dimitrij Oscarovic Ott (1855-1929). Urol Int. 2014;92(4):387-91. doi: 10.1159/000358016. Epub 2014 May 20. PMID: 24852454.).
This trend towards less invasive procedures has continued, with the development of revolutionary robotic surgical systems such as the da Vinci Surgical System in the late 1990s, which has become widely adopted for various surgical procedures (Intuitive Surgical. da Vinci Surgical System. \url{https://www.intuitive.com/en-us/products-and-services/da-vinci/surgical-systems}. Accessed June 2024).

\paragraph{Macro-micro architecture motivation}
The modern frontier of surgical robotics is achieving human-level dexterity,
accuracy, and force control in increasingly small and complex anatomical spaces.
One key technological direction enabling this transition is minimally invasive
surgical tooling with a macro-micro architecture.
The macro unit provides large-workspace positioning and efficiency; however,
its larger structure can be prone to deformation and vibration.
The micro manipulation unit is then attached to the larger arm and provides the
distal dexterity required for manipulation in confined anatomy, where surgical
targets often require end-effectors in the 3--4 mm class for practical access
through narrow channels and natural orifices. In current snake-robot literature,
this micro stage is typically tendon/cable-actuated to achieve high local
curvature and fine orientation control, but its performance depends strongly on
motion transmission quality, sensing, and control architecture (Seetohul and
Shafiee, 2022). In a macro-micro arrangement, this local dexterity complements
the macro arm's gross positioning capability and helps isolate the endpoint from
macro-stage vibration through coordinated control (Scott, 2022).

\paragraph{Cost and accessibility context}
With growing demand and a rapidly expanding surgical robotics market, there is
ongoing debate over whether the high cost of integrated platforms such as da
Vinci limits accessibility, with acquisition costs reported up to \$4 million
AUD and per-procedure costs of roughly \$1000--\$5000 (Do the costs of robotic
surgery present an insurmountable obstacle? A narrative review).
Despite clear clinical benefits, providers must still evaluate cost-effectiveness,
especially in resource-limited settings (Do the costs of robotic surgery present
an insurmountable obstacle? A narrative review).
Evidence from low-to-middle income settings further shows that adoption barriers
are not limited to purchase price alone: recurring maintenance, disposable
instrument costs, and sustained training requirements can prevent programme
continuity even after initial installation (Raza et al., 2024).
The same review also highlights system-level constraints, including limited
infrastructure, workforce training gaps, and tension between investing in
robotics versus addressing unmet basic surgical needs, reinforcing a clear
translational gap for scalable low-cost platforms (Raza et al., 2024).
Taken together, these findings indicate a specific gap: affordable macro-micro
systems that combine low-cost hardware with delay-robust, clinically credible
teleoperation performance are still underdeveloped.
These findings support the broader literature position that low-cost macro-micro
architectures, including off-the-shelf hardware and open-source software stacks,
are an important direction for improving accessibility while maintaining MIS
precision and safety requirements (Scott, 2022).

\paragraph{UR5e baseline and observed limitation}
Specifically, the UR5e is a widely used, cost-effective robotic arm that can
serve as the macro stage, with reported pricing around \$50,000--\$60,000 AUD,
and can be integrated with a range of custom micro systems (Universal Robots
price guide: What to expect (new and used costs)).
The use of a UR5e-based macro-micro architecture has been demonstrated in prior
thesis work by L. Scott (2022), which provided a proof-of-concept teleoperation
pipeline using open-source ROS2.
However, as highlighted by Scott, translational macro-arm commands produced
observable vibration in the integrated system. Reducing this behaviour is
necessary to meet clinical expectations for safety, repeatability, and motion
quality in operating environments.

Continuum tips are a clinically relevant class of hyper-redundant end-effectors for MIS
because they combine high dexterity with slender geometries that can be advanced
through confined access pathways (e.g., lumens, natural orifices, and small
incisions), where conventional rigid instruments are mechanically constrained.
In the tendon-driven subclass, curvature is generated through differential tendon
tension about a central backbone or articulated serial structure, enabling
multi-DOF distal steering with compact proximal actuation and making these devices
well suited to macro-micro architectures (Scott, 2022; Razjigaev et al., 2021;
Seetohul and Shafiee, 2022).

Within this context, current UR5e-based continuum implementations in the local
literature follow the tendon-driven snake-like micro-manipulator direction
established in Lachlan
Scott's thesis, including a 4 mm-class distal tool, macro-micro integration with
a UR5e platform, and teleoperation via a haptic interface. Scott reports
preliminary electromagnetic-tracking validation showing close pose mirroring under
teleoperation (reported average absolute position errors of 0.0272 mm and
0.0161 mm across trial groups), while also identifying practical engineering
constraints that remain unresolved, including macro-stage
translational vibration and the time-consuming exchange of tendon-driven
end-effectors in the current modular workflow (Scott, 2022; Razjigaev et al.,
2021; Razjigaev et al., 2022; Seetohul and Shafiee, 2022).

\subsubsection{Modelling Foundations Relevant to This Thesis}
To situate control and modularity claims on a consistent mathematical basis,
three modelling layers from continuum-robotics literature are directly relevant
to this project.

At the kinematic level, each segment transform can be written as
\begin{equation}
T_i =
\begin{bmatrix}
R_i & p_i \\
\mathbf{0}^\top & 1
\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
which supports forward mapping from joint/segment states to distal pose and is
the baseline representation used across continuum and hyper-redundant modelling
frameworks (Burgner-Kahrs et al., 2015; Dupont et al., 2022).

For tendon-driven discrete continuum segments, piecewise-constant-curvature
formulations remain practical for actuator-to-workspace mapping:
\begin{equation}
T_i^{i+1} =
\mathrm{rot}(Z_i,\alpha_i)\,\mathrm{trans}(Z_i,H_i)\,\mathrm{trans}(X_i,W_i)
\,\mathrm{rot}(Y_i,\theta_i)\,\mathrm{rot}(Z_i,-\alpha_i),
\end{equation}
which is the core structure behind recent high-DOF tendon-driven modelling and
calibration workflows (Wang et al., 2022; Dupont et al., 2022).

When compliance, external loading, and routing-dependent behavior become
dominant, Cosserat-rod equilibrium/dynamics provide the stronger foundation:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{p}_s = \boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{v},\quad
\boldsymbol{R}_s = \boldsymbol{R}\hat{\boldsymbol{u}},\quad
\boldsymbol{n}_s = \rho A\boldsymbol{R}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{q}_t)-\boldsymbol{f},\quad
\boldsymbol{m}_s = (\rho\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{J}\boldsymbol{\omega})_t
-\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}_s\boldsymbol{n}-\boldsymbol{l}.
\end{equation}
This class of models is especially relevant to this thesis where tendon
friction/hysteresis, backlash compensation, and post-swap recalibration are
coupled through compliant routing effects rather than purely rigid kinematics
(Janabi-Sharifi et al., 2021).

In critical terms, the literature indicates that no single layer is sufficient
for workflow-ready surgical performance: kinematic models are computationally
efficient but can under-represent load-dependent effects, while full
mechanics-based models are more expressive but harder to identify and maintain
in real time. This trade-off directly motivates hybrid compensation strategies
in the subsequent teleoperation and modularity sections.

Within this control context, the relevant evidence can be read in five linked
layers: tremor suppression, latency compensation, robust state estimation,
predictive bilateral control, and intent-aware shared autonomy.

Human physiological tremor is a central control challenge in robot-assisted MIS
because involuntary operator motion is transmitted through the teleoperation loop
and can appear as vibration at the surgical instrument tip, degrading precision
during fine tissue manipulation, suturing, and needle handling. Sang et al.
(2016) report that tremor suppression methods must balance two competing
requirements: strong attenuation of involuntary motion and minimal phase delay,
as latency in the command pathway can directly reduce teleoperation quality.
Their zero-phase adaptive fuzzy Kalman filter (ZPAFKF) combines a zero-phase
prefilter with adaptive Kalman estimation to improve tremor estimation and
compensation in real time, and experimental master-slave results show lower
error and reduced tip vibration relative to standard filtering baselines. This
is directly relevant to the present thesis because it frames tremor control not
as a standalone signal-processing problem, but as a coupled teleoperation and
tracking problem in which compensation quality, delay, and manipulator dynamics
must be considered together (Sang et al., 2016).

System latency is a second critical bottleneck in bilateral teleoperation,
particularly when haptic feedback is included. Batty et al. (2022) show that
communication delay creates a fundamental transparency-stability trade-off:
increasing direct force correspondence can degrade stability, while aggressive
stabilisation can mute the fidelity of operator feedback. In response, they
implement an environment-estimation and force-prediction architecture, where
slave-side contact parameters are estimated online (EWRLS) and master feedback
is generated through a predicted virtual environment rather than delayed direct
force reflection. Their human-in-the-loop palpation experiments demonstrate that
this predictive approach substantially reduces force prediction error (with the
Hunt-Crossley model outperforming Kelvin-Voigt and direct force feedback), and
improves teleoperation transparency under delay. A key implication in the
literature is that latency management should be treated as a control-architecture
problem, not solely a communications problem, and that model quality strongly
influences the practical quality of teleoperated manipulation (Batty et al.,
2022).

Filtering is therefore not optional in surgical teleoperation; it is a
foundational requirement for maintaining stable and trustworthy state estimates
when measurements are noisy and network transport is imperfect. Lashari et al.
(2025) show that a Kalman filter paired with MOESP-identified state-space
models can estimate patient-side manipulator position with high robustness under
delay, jitter, and packet loss, while remaining computationally lightweight for
real-time use. This is especially relevant when exact plant dynamics are not
available a priori, because MOESP builds the model directly from surgical
kinematic data (JIGSAWS), allowing filtering performance to be grounded in
observed system behaviour rather than idealised assumptions. In practical terms,
filters are needed to smooth stochastic disturbances, reconstruct missing or
delayed information, and preserve control continuity so that downstream motion
control and haptic/visual feedback remain clinically usable. A key limitation,
however, is that the reported performance is primarily based on dataset-driven
modelling and simulated network impairments, so in vivo generalisability and
cross-platform transfer still require further validation (Lashari et al., 2025).

Predictive bilateral control is also emerging as a practical strategy for
continuum and snake-like teleoperation under delay. Ebrahimian et al. (2024)
propose an online architecture that combines model-mediated force prediction on
the master side with ANN-based operator motion prediction on the slave side,
with the explicit goal of reducing perceived communication lag while preserving
position synchronisation and haptic transparency. This is highly relevant to the
macro-micro literature because it addresses the same core bottleneck as
latency and filtering studies, but at the controller-architecture level: delay
compensation is handled through coordinated forward and backward prediction,
rather than through one-sided compensation only. Their simulation results show
strong delay bypass for known constant delays in snake-like robot teleoperation,
but a key limitation is that performance is demonstrated mainly in simulation
with structured assumptions (e.g., known constant delay and constrained robot
DOFs), which may not fully capture variable-delay clinical networks or
higher-complexity surgical interaction dynamics (Ebrahimian et al., 2024).

Beyond tremor and delay, user intent mapping is a central teleoperation design
problem in shared control. Bowman et al. (2024) argue that direct motion
mapping alone is insufficient for robust telemanipulation when the human and
robot hands are physically dissimilar, because low-level geometric tracking does
not necessarily preserve high-level task success. Their intent-based task-oriented
shared control framework instead blends operator-following behaviour with
autonomous task assistance using inferred intent probabilities and adaptive
arbitration weights. Critically, this contribution is not just another blending
policy: it formalises how authority should shift based on hand-structure
discrepancy and task constraints, which is directly relevant to macro-micro MIS
systems where distal tools cannot perfectly mimic surgeon hand kinematics. This
supports the broader literature position that intuitive teleoperation should be evaluated by
both task completion and perceived control, rather than endpoint tracking error
alone (Bowman et al., 2024).

However, an important limitation is that the reported validation is constrained
to controlled grasping tasks and specific intent models, so transferability to
highly dynamic, deformable, and safety-critical in vivo surgical contexts remains
an open question.

Transmission backlash in cable-pulley drives is another directly relevant
control bottleneck for laparoscopic end-effectors because direction reversals
create delay and transition phases in which distal motion/torque do not track
proximal commands linearly. Zou et al. (2022) model this explicitly and use a
feedforward compensation term linked to output-side backlash,
$\theta_{comp}=\Delta\theta_{out}\,R_5/R_0$, together with grasping torque
estimation and saturation limiting, $M_{grasp}=M_{out}-M_{fric}$, to reduce
tracking error and limit unsafe distal force. Their results indicate meaningful
improvements in position and force control for cable-pulley laparoscopic
instruments: reported distal-grasper position error is reduced to approximately
1.03$^\circ$ mean (3.49$^\circ$ max), and grasping torque tracking error is
reported below 0.025 N$\cdot$m in their compensated instrument tests. Nonetheless,
residual error around direction reversal remains, highlighting that compensation
quality still depends on parameter identification and friction-model fidelity.

Related tendon--sheath evidence further strengthens this point in shape-variable
conditions. Hong et al. (2025) model backlash-like hysteresis in tendon--sheath
pairs using an equivalent-circle representation, where deadband is linked to
master/slave deformation mismatch ($D=\delta_{TSM(master)}-
\delta_{TSM(slave)}$) and then compensated by feedforward updated from
proximal calibration without distal sensing. Their reported results show RMSE
reduction from 1.31 mm to 0.19 mm on average (85.5\% reduction). This is
highly relevant to laparoscopic routing uncertainty; however, the approach still
depends on reliable calibration and assumptions about unchanged geometric
conditions during each compensation interval.

Collectively, these control studies establish clear performance requirements for
low-cost macro-micro platforms and provide a direct conceptual bridge to modular
continuum hardware discussions.

\subsection{Modularity and Tendon-Driven Continuum Manipulators}
In this review, a \textit{monolithic} distal architecture is defined as a single
integrated tool assembly in which changing function requires major teardown or
full distal module replacement rather than rapid local exchange. This framing is
consistent with the architecture-level distinction between integrated and
modular robotic platforms reported by Cowan et al. (2024).

The practical consequences are workflow-critical: longer swap time,
re-tensioning and re-calibration burden after replacement, interruption of the
operative sequence, and reduced intraoperative adaptability when task needs
change mid-procedure. These constraints align with broader integration-friction
evidence in modular end-effector software/hardware pipelines (Torielli et al.,
2023), where even technically feasible modularity often carries non-trivial
changeover overhead.

Internal platform observations in the prior local UR5e-continuum implementation
also indicate that end-effector replacement is time-consuming and introduces
practical workflow friction around repeatable setup after each swap (Scott,
2022). Together, the literature and local prior-work evidence indicate that
architectural modularity alone is insufficient unless exchange-time and
post-swap recalibration costs are explicitly addressed.

\subsubsection{Tool Exchange Mechanisms}
Tool exchange is necessary because laparoscopic workflows require multiple
functional instruments within one procedure, while only a limited subset can be
mounted simultaneously on the robotic arm (Ko et al., 2025). This creates a
recurrent intraoperative transition task that can interrupt surgical flow and,
when assistant-dependent, introduces operator-assistant coordination variance.

At present, the strongest direct evidence remains with rail-guided,
cartridge-like exchange approaches. Ko et al. (2025) analyse current sliding
exchange practice and report an automated replacement architecture designed to
preserve trocar/RCM constraints via linear motion. Their prototype-level results
report full exchange in 15 s, reduction from an initial 27 s cycle, and a total
device mass of 3.36 kg (below their 4 kg design target), indicating feasible
automation under laparoscopic geometric constraints.

Alternative strategies aim to reduce exchange frequency rather than improve
exchange interfaces directly. Mocellin et al. (2026) present OriGrasp as a
reconfigurable multifunctional end-effector (introducer mode and compliant
grasper mode), validated through dVRK integration and tissue-interaction tests.
Reported performance includes approximately 4 N pinch force and bowel pulling/
lifting forces in literature-consistent atraumatic ranges. This supports
multifunctionality as a useful partial mitigation for selected abdominal tasks.
However, current evidence remains task-specific and does not demonstrate that a
single reconfigurable device can replace the broader instrument set required
across heterogeneous procedures.

Mechanism diversity beyond rail-guided exchange remains under-validated in the
reviewed evidence base. Direct surgical evidence is limited for bayonet
quarter-turn, cam-latch, cartridge-head variants beyond linear sliding, and
kinematic-coupling-assisted docking with active preload. Reported gaps are not
purely mechanical: tolerance sensitivity, sterilisation robustness,
wear-dependent repeatability drift, and post-reconnection re-zeroing burden all
remain insufficiently quantified across repeated swap cycles.

Control evidence indicates that docking success alone is insufficient for
workflow-ready exchange. Cable-pulley and tendon-sheath studies report
backlash/hysteresis and force-estimation sensitivity to pretension, friction,
and routing geometry (Zou et al., 2022; Hong et al., 2025). In particular,
Hong et al. (2025) show geometry-dependent deadband behavior and report RMSE
reduction from 1.31 mm to 0.19 mm with feedforward compensation after
calibration, implying that post-swap recovery may require both tension
re-establishment and compensation-state updates when routing changes.

For post-swap tension management specifically, Singh and Khatait (2022) provide
relevant but indirect evidence via an RCM tension-adjustment mechanism enabling
independent cable pretension tuning with invariant routed tendon length. The key
limitation is translational scope: this is not a direct quick-change end-effector
interface study and does not quantify repeated dock-undock surgical workflow
performance.

\textbf{Critical judgement and knowledge gap.} The literature supports a clear
hierarchy of maturity: (i) strongest evidence for linear, rail-guided exchange,
(ii) promising but task-limited multifunctional substitution, and (iii) weak
comparative evidence for alternative docking/locking families. The unresolved
research gap is therefore the integrated validation of \emph{mechanism + control
recovery} after repeated swaps: exchange time, docking repeatability,
post-swap backlash/deadband, retensioning effort, and force-safety consistency
must be quantified together under clinically realistic routing and sterilisation
conditions.

\subsection{System Safety and Reliability Considerations}
This section is structured as a staged argument from hazard definition to
quantified residual risk, so that the final gap is explicit and testable.

\subsubsection{3.1 Safety-Critical Hazard Model for Macro-Micro MIS}
\textit{Purpose:} Define what can go wrong and what must be bounded.
\begin{itemize}
    \item enumerate hazard classes: unintended contact, excessive distal force, delayed response, post-swap miscalibration, and control-state mismatch after direction reversal,
    \item state measurable safety variables: distal force/torque limits, position error, response latency, and allowed deadband/backlash window,
    \item link each hazard to literature-supported mechanism/control causes.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{3.2 Distal Contact and Force-Limiting Strategies}
\textit{Purpose:} Show how current work constrains tissue interaction risk.
\begin{itemize}
    \item compare force-limiting approaches: intrinsic compliance, model-based force estimation, and torque saturation,
    \item extract quantitative thresholds/results where available (e.g., force-tracking and grasp-safety outcomes),
    \item identify missing evidence: transfer from benchtop/tissue-phantom performance to clinically variable in vivo conditions.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{3.3 Teleoperation Reliability Under Delay, Jitter, and Tremor}
\textit{Purpose:} Establish reliability limits of the command loop itself.
\begin{itemize}
    \item synthesise predictive control, filtering, and intent arbitration literature as complementary layers,
    \item assess robustness to delay and packet-loss assumptions versus real operating-network variability,
    \item identify unresolved issue: consistent safety-performance under simultaneous delay, model mismatch, and human adaptation.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{3.4 Backlash/Hysteresis and Compensation Stability}
\textit{Purpose:} Connect transmission nonlinearity directly to safety and repeatability.
\begin{itemize}
    \item review backlash-like hysteresis models for cable-pulley and tendon-sheath paths,
    \item summarise compensation mathematics and reported residual errors,
    \item identify reliability gap at direction reversals and post-calibration drift.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{3.5 Tool-Exchange Safety Recovery}
\textit{Purpose:} Treat swap events as safety-critical state transitions, not only mechanical operations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item define post-swap recovery requirements: tension re-establishment, deadband update, re-zeroing, and force-limit consistency,
    \item evaluate what exchange studies quantify versus what they omit (repeated-cycle degradation, sterilisation-driven wear, and cumulative calibration burden),
    \item identify coupling gap: mechanism docking success does not guarantee control-state safety restoration.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{3.6 Fault Handling and Safe-State Behavior}
\textit{Purpose:} Evaluate how systems fail, not only how they track.
\begin{itemize}
    \item synthesise evidence on safe-stop logic, bounded fallback behavior, and operator override pathways,
    \item map expected failure modes (sensor dropouts, cable slack, communication interruption) to mitigation strategies,
    \item identify missing quantitative benchmarks for recovery time and residual error after fault events.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{3.7 Reliability Evidence Across Lifecycle}
\textit{Purpose:} Determine whether reported performance is durable.
\begin{itemize}
    \item review evidence for repeatability across prolonged trials, repeated swaps, and changing pretension states,
    \item include sterilisation/contamination and wear effects where available,
    \item identify the dominant gap: limited long-horizon, clinically representative reliability datasets for low-cost macro-micro platforms.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{3.8 Section-Level Critical Synthesis and Gap Statement}
\textit{Purpose:} Conclude with a single defendable knowledge gap.
\begin{itemize}
    \item synthesise that current literature validates isolated safety mechanisms but rarely validates integrated safety recovery after reconfiguration,
    \item state thesis-relevant gap: jointly validated \emph{mechanism + control + recovery} safety performance under realistic routing, delay, and swap conditions,
    \item define candidate evaluation endpoints for later chapters: post-swap recovery time, residual deadband/backlash, distal-force limit adherence, and task-level repeatability.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Synthesis of Literature and Knowledge Gaps}
Across recent literature, there is strong agreement that high-quality surgical
teleoperation depends on coordinated management of tremor, delay,
state-estimation uncertainty, and operator intent. However, most validated
architectures are demonstrated either on high-cost integrated systems or under
simplifying assumptions (e.g., known constant delay, controlled tasks, or
simulation-only evaluation), which limits generalisability to resource-constrained
and clinically variable settings.

In parallel, continuum and snake-like end-effectors provide clear distal dexterity
advantages for confined MIS workspaces, but persistent control difficulties remain
around tendon friction, hysteresis, backlash, calibration drift, and repeatability
after hardware reconfiguration. Evidence from low-to-middle income contexts also
shows that long-term adoption depends not only on capital cost but on maintenance,
disposables, training continuity, and infrastructure support.

Taken together, the most consequential knowledge gaps are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item integrated control frameworks that retain transparency and stability under realistic communication imperfections,
    \item reproducible evaluation protocols for low-cost macro-micro systems using clinically meaningful performance metrics,
    \item robust, repeatable modular continuum interfaces that minimise post-swap uncertainty,
    \item long-horizon reliability evidence (wear, sterilisation, recalibration burden) for tendon-driven reconfigurable tools.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Priority Evidence Gaps for Additional Review}
The review can be strengthened further by adding recent studies on:
\begin{itemize}
    \item benchmark datasets and standardised test protocols for continuum surgical teleoperation,
    \item variable-delay and packet-loss robust bilateral/shared control in human-in-the-loop settings,
    \item safety assurance frameworks (fault handling, bounded motion, force-limited behaviour) for MIS robots,
    \item quantitative studies of modular tool-exchange repeatability and calibration drift,
    \item lifecycle reliability of tendon-driven instruments under repeated use and sterilisation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
The reviewed literature shows a clear convergence on three points. First,
macro-micro architectures and tendon-driven continuum tools are well motivated
for MIS dexterity, but remain sensitive to vibration transmission, modelling
error, and teleoperation instability. Second, modern control strategies
(filtering, latency compensation, predictive bilateral control, and intent-aware
shared autonomy) improve performance, yet are often validated under constrained
assumptions that limit direct transfer to clinically variable settings. Third,
cost and adoption studies indicate that accessibility is governed by full-system
burden (maintenance, disposables, training continuity, and infrastructure), not
capital cost alone.

Taken together, the strongest unresolved knowledge gaps are integrated,
reproducible frameworks that jointly address delay-robust control, modular
reconfiguration repeatability, safety-critical reliability, and realistic
cost-constrained deployment conditions.
